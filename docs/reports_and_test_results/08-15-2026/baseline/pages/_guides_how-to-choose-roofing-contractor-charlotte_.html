Your roof is one of the biggest things you'll pay for as a homeowner, and picking who does the work matters more than picking the shingle. The hard part is that every roofing company's website says the same things, and after a big storm your street fills up with trucks you've never seen before. This guide covers what's actually worth asking — proof of insurance, a scope in writing, what the warranty really covers, and the red flags that should end the conversation. Safe Haven is a fully insured, Charlotte-based roofer with six years on local roofs, and we'd rather you ask us all of this than not.

Ask for proof of insurance

Roofing is dangerous work, and it happens on your property. People are up on a steep surface with nail guns, tear-off coming off the edge, and if something goes wrong up there the question of who carries that risk stops being abstract fast. So ask any roofer you’re considering to show you proof they’re insured — then actually look at it. Read the company name. Check the dates. Plenty of homeowners ask, get a confident yes, and never see a thing, which isn’t really the same as asking. What your own homeowner’s policy covers is a conversation for you and your agent, not us. What we can tell you is that Safe Haven is fully insured, and we’re glad to hand you the proof before we ever set foot on your roof. A roofer who gets cagey when you ask has answered you.

Get the scope in writing

A number said out loud in your driveway isn’t a quote. It’s a guess you can’t hold anyone to. What you want is the scope — the actual list of what will happen to your roof, written down before anyone starts. That means the material and the specific line, not just “architectural shingles.” It means what’s coming off: how many layers, and who hauls them away. It means what happens if the crew finds soft or rotted decking under the old shingles, because that’s common, and it’s better to know the plan now than to sort it out with a crew in your yard. It means the details — flashing, valleys, pipe boots, drip edge. And it means cleanup, down to who runs the magnet over your grass. Things can still change once a roof is open, but a written scope means you both know what was agreed to.

Ask what the warranty actually covers

“Lifetime warranty” on a yard sign means very little until you ask which warranty, because there are two. The manufacturer’s warranty covers the materials — if the shingles themselves fail the way they weren’t supposed to, that’s the manufacturer’s, and those terms come from them, not your roofer. The workmanship warranty covers the installation: the nailing, the flashing, the way the valleys were cut. That’s the roofer’s own promise about their own work. Most leaks in a young roof trace back to workmanship rather than materials, which is why that one deserves the harder questions. Ask what it covers, how long it runs, and get it in writing alongside the scope. Ours is five years, on top of whatever material warranty comes with the shingles you pick. Any warranty is worth exactly as much as the company standing behind it.

Watch out for storm chasers

After a bad hailstorm, Charlotte streets fill with trucks nobody recognizes. Some of those crews are good. Some are storm chasers — outfits that follow weather from state to state, knock every door on the block hard, push for a signature that same afternoon, and are three states away by the time something on your roof needs a second look. Be fair about this. A roofer knocking after a storm isn’t automatically a bad one, and plenty of solid local companies work a neighborhood that just got hit. The tell isn’t the knock. It’s the pressure, the vagueness, and the address on the truck. Ask where they’re based. Ask who you’d be calling in two years. Because a five-year workmanship warranty, or any warranty, is a promise from a company — and a promise is only as good as that company still being reachable when you need it.

Local track record matters

Roofing companies come and go, so how long one has actually worked your area tells you something real. Safe Haven has spent six years on Charlotte-area roofs. We’re based here, our crews drive home to the same metro they work in, and that shows up in the small stuff: what our summers do to asphalt, how a hail line runs through certain neighborhoods, what an older Charlotte roof deck looks like once the shingles come off. Ask any roofer to show you real local work. Not stock photos — actual roofs, in towns you recognize. Ours are in the gallery, and our Charlotte roofing page covers what we do across the metro. A company working the same streets year after year has a name it has to keep, and that’s a kind of accountability an out-of-state truck doesn’t carry.
