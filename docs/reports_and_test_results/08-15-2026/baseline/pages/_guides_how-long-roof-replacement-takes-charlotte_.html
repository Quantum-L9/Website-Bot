It's a fair question, and you deserve better than the answer most sites give you. Roofers love to say a roof takes a day — but the honest truth is that nobody can tell you how long your roof will take without looking at it first. A simple roof on a one-story ranch and a steep, cut-up roof with two layers to tear off are not the same job. This guide lays out exactly what moves the timeline, so you know what you're planning around and what questions to ask. Safe Haven is a fully insured, Charlotte-based roofer with six years on local roofs, and the inspection that gets you a real timeline is free.

Why a day count is the wrong question

Ask five roofers how long your roof will take and you’ll get five numbers, every one of them offered before anybody climbed a ladder. Those aren’t measurements. They’re sales answers — the thing you wanted to hear, said fast, to keep the conversation moving.

The trouble isn’t that the number is wrong. It’s that you’ll plan around it. You’ll take off work, move the cars, warn the neighbors. Then the crew pulls the old roof back, finds something nobody could see from the driveway, and your plan slides.

Two roofs can measure out to the same square footage and still be completely different work — one plain and walkable, the other steep and cut up with two layers to come off. So the number isn’t really what you’re after. What you want is a timeline you can plan around: built from your roof, with the real variables already accounted for. That takes someone getting on it first.

Roof size and complexity

Roofers measure in squares — one square is 100 square feet — and it’s the first thing we count, because more roof means more of everything. More material up the ladder, more labor across the deck, more debris back down.

Then there’s pitch. A low, walkable roof lets a crew move naturally and work at a steady clip. A steep one doesn’t. Steep means harnesses, staging, and shorter safe stretches of work, and every bundle of shingles gets moved with more care. Nobody rushes on a steep roof, and you wouldn’t want them to.

After that it’s shape. A plain gable with two clean planes is the simplest roof there is. Start adding valleys where planes meet, a chimney to flash around, skylights to seal, dormers to cut in — and every one of those is careful hand work that a wide-open deck never asks for.

Tear-off and what’s underneath

Before the new roof goes on, the old one comes off, and how much old roof there is matters. One layer of shingles is a straightforward strip. Two layers is twice the tearing, twice the weight, and twice the hauling — all of it down off the roof, into a dumpster, and out to the landfill. That’s real work, and it happens before a single new shingle gets nailed.

The bigger variable is the part nobody can see yet. Under the shingles is the decking, the plywood everything is nailed to. From the ground it looks the same whether it’s solid or soft. We don’t know until the old roof is off and we’re standing on it. If we find plywood gone spongy from a leak that’s been running quietly for years, that decking gets replaced — a new roof nailed to rotted wood isn’t a new roof.

Weather and permits in Charlotte

Weather is the variable nobody controls, and here it isn’t a small one. Charlotte summers build storms out of a clear morning. That matters more than it sounds like it should, because a roof mid-replacement is an open roof — the old covering is gone and the deck is exposed. We don’t open up a roof we can’t get dried in. If the forecast turns, we’d rather hold a start date than tear off in front of a storm and hand you a wet ceiling. Sometimes that means a job moves.

Permits are the other piece. Roof replacements around here are permitted and inspected work — your new roof gets built to code and signed off. It’s a normal part of every replacement we do, and it’s a real step with its own timing that belongs on an honest schedule instead of being quietly left off.

Getting a real timeline for your roof

Here’s how you actually get the answer. We come out and get on the roof: the squares, the pitch, the valleys and penetrations, how many layers are up there, what the decking sounds like underfoot. We look in the attic if we can, because that’s where an old leak tells on itself. Then we write it up.

What comes back to you is a quote and a timeline together, before you’ve committed to anything. Where there’s a piece we can’t be certain of — and decking is usually that piece — we’ll say so plainly and tell you what happens if we find it, rather than discovering it out loud on the day.

Every roof replacement we do carries our five-year workmanship warranty alongside the manufacturer’s material warranty on the shingles themselves. The inspection is free, with no obligation attached to it.

How Long Does a Roof Replacement Take? — FAQ

How long does a roof replacement take?+

It depends on the size and pitch of your roof, how many old layers come off, and what we find in the decking underneath — plus weather and permit timing. We'll walk you through the timeline for your roof before we start.

Why won't you just tell me a number?+

Because a number we made up before seeing your roof isn't worth much. A simple, walkable roof and a steep one with two layers and rotted decking are genuinely different jobs. We'd rather look at your actual roof and give you a timeline we can stand behind.

What can delay a roof replacement?+

Weather is the big one — we don't open up a roof we can't dry in. Beyond that: rotted decking we find once the old roof is off, a steep or complicated roof that slows the work down safely, and permit timing.

Do I need to be home while you work?+

No. Most of what we need is access to the roof and the driveway. We'll agree on the details before the day so there aren't surprises.

When will I know my timeline?+

After the free inspection. Once we've been on your roof and know what we're dealing with, we'll give you a clear timeline along with your quote, before you commit to anything.
